1. Chapter 1: Introduction (slides 1–14)

1.1 What is an Information System (IS)? (slide 3)

Definition (course: learn it word for word) An information system represents the set of elements involved in the management, storage, processing, transmission and dissemination of information within an organization.

Its 4 roles (slide 3):

  1. Collection of information (gathering it),
  2. Storage (keeping it, e.g. a database),
  3. Processing (computing, transforming it),
  4. Dissemination (sending it to the people who need it).

Simple example: your school's IS

  • Collection: students fill in the registration form.
  • Storage: students and grades saved in the database.
  • Processing: averages computed automatically.
  • Dissemination: transcripts sent / posted on the platform.

An IS is not only computers: people, procedures and data are part of it too. The computer part is the "computerized system" we model.

1.4 Modeling, model, method, language (slides 8–9)

Modeling = support for design: formalizing the solution with known notations/rules. It reduces complexity: remove non-significant details, keep what matters to understand and predict.

⚠ CarefulSlide 9 lists "Merise, UML" as examples of design methods, but UML is a modeling language, not a method (the "L" = Language). It says how to draw, not which steps to follow. If asked, say: "UML is a modeling language; Merise is a method."

UML = Unified Modeling Language. Used in information systems, telecoms, transport, aeronautics, aerospace, science, and even to model mechanical or human behaviours.

3 basic building blocks (slide 13):

  • Elements: the essential abstractions of the model (actor, class, use case…).
  • Relationships: the links between elements (association, include, inheritance…).
  • Diagrams: visual representations of the elements; each gives a partial view from one perspective (user, admin…). All diagrams together give the overall view of the system.

UML diagram types (slide 14): 14 diagrams in 2 families

Structural (static) = 7Behavioral (dynamic) = 7

Trap: the use case diagram is behavioral, not structural. Class and object diagrams are structural.

2. Chapter 2: the Use Case diagram (slides 1–12)

2.1 What it is (slide 3)

  • The functional UML diagram (the functional axis of §1.2).
  • A way to specify the uses / features of a system: what the system does for its users, not how.
  • Represents the interactions between users and the system.
  • A graphical representation (the diagram) + a textual description (the documentation, §2.6).

2.2 The basic elements (slides 4–5)

{{fig:uc_basic}}
ElementCourse definitionHow to recognise it / write it
Actor (stick figure)Role played by any external entity that interacts with the system. • It's a role, not a person: Ahmed can be a "Student" and a "Customer". 5 employees with the same job = one actor "Employee".
• It can be a human or another system (e.g. "Personnel management system", "Bank"). Put it outside the rectangle.
• (extra) Primary actor = starts the use case to get a goal (usually on the left); secondary actor = is asked for help by the system (usually on the right).

• A goal that gives a result of value to the actor, not a button click or a technical step.
System (rectangle)The boundary of the system (title = system name).Use cases inside, actors outside.

2.3 The 4 relationships (slides 4–7): the heart of the chapter

{{fig:uc_rel}}
RelationshipCourse definitionNotation & arrow directionKey words in the text
Interaction
(association)		Relationship between an actor and a UC.Plain solid line (optional arrow, §2.5)."The member can borrow…"
IncludeA UC contains the actions defined in another UC.Dashed arrow «include» from the base UC → the included UC. The included one is always executed."must be preceded by", "requires", "always", "each time… the system checks"
ExtendA UC can be extended by another UC.Dashed arrow «extend» from the optional UC → the base UC. The extension happens only sometimes (a condition)."optionally", "may", "can also", "in case of", "if…"
GeneralizationA UC is a specification of another UC; an actor is a specification of another actor.Solid line + hollow triangle pointing to the general one (child → parent)."there are 2 kinds of…", "can be X or Y", "an engineer is an employee who can also…"
💡 Include vs extend: the trick to never confuse the arrows Include: read the arrow as "needs": "Log in needs Verify authentication". The arrow leaves the one who needs.
Extend: read the arrow as "can be added to": "Display error can be added to Verify authentication". The arrow leaves the optional one.
In both cases the arrow points towards the main/base story for extend, and towards the reusable piece for include.
⚠ Actor generalization (very used on the board) If the child actor (Engineer, Customer, Head teacher) can do everything the parent does (Employee, Visitor, Teacher) plus more → triangle from child to parent. The child then inherits all the parent's use cases: don't draw the parent's lines again from the child.

2.4 Multiplicities (refinement concepts 1–2, slides 8–9)

{{fig:uc_mult}}
  • At the UC end (e.g. 1..n near "Transfer funds"): the actor is involved in several instances of this UC: in parallel, by mutual exclusion, or at different times (UML doesn't say which).
  • At the actor end (e.g. 2..n near "Player"): several instances of the actor are needed to perform one UC: simultaneous actions, or complementary and successive actions.

2.5 Interaction orientation (refinement concept 3, slide 10)

An arrow on the actor–UC line shows the direction of the interaction (who starts / who receives): "User → Sign in" (the user triggers it), "Display balance → User" (the system sends to the user). No arrow = bidirectional. In the board corrections, the prof often puts the arrow from the actor to the UC.

3. Method: solving a use case exercise in the exam

3.1 The 7 steps (do them in this order, on the draft first)

  1. Read the whole text once without writing. Find the name of the system (it becomes the rectangle title).
  2. Underline the actors: the nouns of people / roles / external systems that do something or are contacted (employee, manager, engineer, bank, "personnel management system"). Ignore things the system manages (books, products: those are data, future classes).
  3. Circle the actions: the verbs "the X can/must…" → each becomes a use case named verb + complement.
  4. Make a table actor → use cases (2 columns). This is the skeleton; it already earns most marks.
  5. Find the relationships with the key words (table §3.2): include, extend, generalization of actors, generalization of UCs.
  6. Draw: rectangle with the title; primary actors on the left, secondary actors on the right; group the UCs of each actor near it; avoid crossing lines.
  7. Check with the list of mistakes (§3.4): every requirement of the text is covered, no arrow between UCs without a stereotype, arrows in the right direction.

3.3 How to lay out the drawing so it's readable

  • Draw the rectangle big (whole page width). Write the system name at the top.
  • Put the actor who has the most UCs on the left middle; put the UCs that only he uses in a column next to him.
  • Put the included / extending UCs further right, so dashed arrows go left→right and don't cross the actor lines.
  • Put the children of a generalization in a small column next to their parent (like "Manage members" → Add / Update / Delete on the board).
  • Write «include» / «extend» on every dashed arrow, and the condition in brackets if the text gives one: «extend» [late > 3 weeks].

3.4 The classic mistakes (= the answer to "critique this diagram")

Pitfalls to look for (and to avoid yourself)
  1. No system boundary (rectangle missing).
  2. Sequencing / flowchart: arrows between UCs to show an order ("log in → search → check out"). A use case diagram shows no order and no flow; that's for activity/sequence diagrams.
  3. Arrows between UCs without a stereotype: every UC–UC link must be «include», «extend» or a generalization.
  4. Too fine granularity / functional decomposition: one UC per tiny operation (add member, modify member, delete member each tied to the actor) instead of one "Manage members" (possibly with children).
  5. Technical steps as use cases: "Log in", "Click OK", "Save to database" are not goals by themselves (authentication = a precondition or an included UC).
  6. Missing requirements: a function of the text has no UC (e.g. legal proceedings, loans).
  7. Wrong actors: data shown as actors (a book), or a person instead of a role, or the system itself as an actor.
  8. Wrong arrow direction for include/extend, or confusing them.
  9. Bad names: no verb, mixed languages ("return oeuvre"), inconsistent style.
  10. Crossing, tangled lines that make it impossible to see which actor does what.

Exercise 3: Media library: critique and correct

Q1: Critique: the pitfalls this student fell into
  1. No system boundary (no rectangle "Media library system").
  2. Sequencing: "log in → Search member → check out a work" are linked by plain arrows to show an order of steps. A use case diagram doesn't model order/flow.

  9. Tangled lines: crossing connectors make it impossible to see which UC belongs to the actor.

💡 How to find a multiplicity: ask the question twice "One Customer places how many Orders?" → 0 or many → write 0..* next to Order.
"One Order is placed by how many Customers?" → exactly 1 → write 1 next to Customer.
The number always goes on the side of the class you're counting.

Association name: a verb in the middle of the line ("places", "makes", "works for").

5.3 Object diagram

A snapshot of real objects at one moment, following a class diagram: box title objectName : ClassName (underlined), attributes with values (name = "Ali"), lines = links (instances of associations), no multiplicities, no operations. Example: TD2 Ex3 Q2 (§6.4).

5.4 Method for a class diagram exercise

  1. Nouns → classes or attributes. A noun with its own information (Teacher, Reservation, Room) = a class. A simple value (name, date, capacity, price) = an attribute.
  2. "is a / can be X or Y / there are 2 kinds of" → generalization (Programmer and Designer are Developers; Student and Teacher are Users).
  3. Verbs between two classes → associations ("a teacher makes reservations", "a team works for a company").
  4. "is composed of / is made of / contains" → composition (the part dies with the whole: board → cells) or aggregation (the part can live alone: team → developers).
  5. Multiplicities: ask the question twice for every association (§5.2). Numbers in the text ("three wheels", "8 neighbours") become multiplicities.
  6. Many-to-many with data (a grade, a quantity, a date of the pair) → association class.
  7. Operations: the verbs the class itself does (uncover(), mark(), compute()). The text's rules become operations.
  8. Check: every noun of the text is somewhere; no attribute repeated in subclasses; types are correct.

7. Exam-day checklist

Before drawing (5 min per exercise)
  • Underline actors / circle verbs / box key words (preceded by, optionally, can be, all, only, composed of).
  • Write the actor → UC table on the draft.
  • For a class diagram: list classes, attributes and verbs between classes.
While drawing
  • Pencil first, big rectangle, system name.
  • Every dashed arrow labelled «include»/«extend», in the right direction.
  • Generalization = hollow triangle towards the parent.
  • Class diagram: multiplicities on both ends of every association.
Final check (2 min)
  • Is every sentence of the text covered by something in the diagram?
  • No arrows between UCs without a stereotype? No flowchart?
  • Actors outside, UCs inside, names = verb + complement?
  • No attribute repeated in a subclass; types String/int/double/Date/boolean?
  • Object diagram: underlined name : Class, values, no multiplicities, consistent with the class diagram?
Write 1–2 lines to justify your choices e.g. "Engineer specializes Employee because engineers can also consult products". Teachers give marks for correct reasoning even if the drawing differs from theirs: in UML there's often more than one correct answer.
